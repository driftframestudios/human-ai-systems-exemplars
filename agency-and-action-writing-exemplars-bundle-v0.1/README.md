# Agency and Action Writing

This compact companion preserves a reusable editorial distinction: when prose hides the work, restore the actor, actual action, object, and consequence. The result is direct and professionally grounded while leaving room for narrative, technical, collective, warm, concise, or reflective voice.

This collection guides judgment rather than fixing a house voice. It also avoids a controlled vocabulary. Words such as *surface*, *field*, *route*, and *model* remain useful when they name real relationships or boundaries; a revision becomes valuable when it says more precisely what is happening.

## Tone goal

Readers can identify:

1. **Actor:** who or what acted;
2. **Action:** what they actually did;
3. **Object:** what the action changed, created, examined, or decided;
4. **Consequence:** what became possible, different, or known; and
5. **Boundary:** what the claim establishes and where agency was shared, constrained, or held elsewhere.

Directness here means semantic clarity. It does not require intimate disclosure, emotional confession, invented motive, or exaggerated ownership. First person is useful when a person’s judgment or contribution matters. Team, role, system, and process constructions remain available when they tell the truth more accurately.

## Fit boundaries

This method fits when the source already establishes an actor and action but the prose obscures them. It also fits when a broad label makes an interface, workflow, artifact, or decision hard to recognize.

An impersonal construction can remain the stronger choice when:

- the actor is unknown, protected, immaterial, or distributed;
- the affected person or object properly belongs first;
- the sentence describes a process whose mechanism matters more than its operator;
- a discipline uses an abstract term to name a real technical or decision boundary; or
- exact quotation, accessibility language, or governed terminology must remain unchanged.

When the source omits a needed actor, action, object, or consequence, make the smallest faithful revision and return the missing fact as a question to the source owner. Public prose leaves the gap open until evidence resolves it.

## A contextual test for abstraction

Ask what the term contributes.

- If *surface* means “some part of the work,” name the page, interface, queue, workflow, prototype, contract, or operating model.
- If *surface* names the place where information, authority, and action meet, retain it. A phrase such as “specialist decision-making surface” can be exact.
- If the reader may not know the concrete referent, pair them: “the genomic review interface, a specialist decision-making surface for pathologists.”

The editorial unit is the sentence and its reader job, not the isolated word.

## Relationship to Semantic Attractor Design

The desired construction stays prominent: name the actor, action, object, and consequence. A boundary also names its useful replacement—for example, “preserve technical *surface* where it marks a real boundary; use *interface* where the sentence means a visible application view.” This posture supports contextual judgment without making the discouraged wording the center of the instruction.

## Completion questions

- Can a reader say who or what did the work?
- Does the main verb name the actual action?
- Is the object concrete enough for this audience?
- Does the consequence match the available evidence?
- Are shared agency, authority, and uncertainty still truthful?
- Does any retained abstraction add boundary meaning?
- Does the voice fit the artifact without sounding confessional or mechanically active?
