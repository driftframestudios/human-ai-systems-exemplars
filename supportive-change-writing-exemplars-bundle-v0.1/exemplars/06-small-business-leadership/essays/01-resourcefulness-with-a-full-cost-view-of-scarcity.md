# Resourcefulness with a Full-Cost View of Scarcity

### Begin with available means and disciplined uncertainty

Conventional planning starts with a desired endpoint and works backward to the resources required. That logic is valuable when demand, technology, and costs are reasonably predictable. Small-business leaders often face a different problem: several futures are plausible, relevant information has yet to emerge, and committing too early could exhaust the very resources needed to adapt. McMullen and Shepherd (2006) distinguish uncertainty from calculable risk and argue that entrepreneurial action depends both on perceived uncertainty and willingness to bear it. The useful question becomes: “What can I responsibly do while important things remain unknown?”

Sarasvathy’s (2001) theory of effectuation offers one answer. An entrepreneur begins with available means—who they are, what they know, and whom they know—and explores outcomes those means can support. Commitments from customers, partners, and suppliers gradually shape the opportunity. Planning enters in sequence: a detailed forecast earns influence as uncertainty becomes tractable; before that point, affordable experiments and negotiated commitments protect room to maneuver.

Self-efficacy matters here, but it should be understood precisely. Chen, Greene, and Crick (1998) found differences in entrepreneurial self-efficacy between founders and managers and connected the construct to task domains. Confidence is most useful when it is specific: the belief that one can conduct five customer interviews, renegotiate a term, learn a new channel, or ask an experienced peer for help. Global declarations of invincibility provide less guidance. A leader can have confidence in the ability to learn while remaining open to what the learning will reveal.

### Recombine resources—and record their true cost

Baker and Nelson (2005) described entrepreneurial bricolage as making do by applying combinations of resources at hand to new problems and opportunities. In a small firm, that may mean adapting existing equipment, cross-training a colleague, sharing space, turning a customer conversation into product insight, or building a modest service around an underused capability. Bricolage expands the field of action because it treats resources as constructed through use, beyond their original labels.

Yet ingenuity has a shadow price. The spare room may be someone’s only quiet space. The colleague who “can handle one more thing” may already be carrying invisible coordination work. Deferred maintenance, unpaid founder labor, lenient supplier terms, and family care can make an enterprise appear efficient by moving costs off its accounts. Financial bootstrapping research documents the varied resource-acquisition practices small-business managers use (Winborg & Landström, 2001); the evidence is descriptive, while responsible use requires attention to who carries each cost.

Resourceful leadership therefore keeps a second ledger. Alongside cash, inventory, and hours, it tracks favors owed, attention fragmented, recovery postponed, dependencies concentrated, and risks transferred to people with less bargaining power. A temporary workaround should have an owner, a review date, and an explicit condition for retirement. If the workaround becomes routine, its full cost belongs in the business model.

Slack is equally nuanced. Bradley, Wiklund, and Shepherd (2011) found that resource slack can operate as a double-edged sword in entrepreneurial management and growth. Too little slack leaves too little capacity to absorb a delay or test an idea; undisciplined slack can weaken focus. A small reserve of cash, time, supplier alternatives, or cross-trained capability is adaptive capacity. The useful reserve protects learning and continuity and earns its place through that contribution.

### Build a network that can disagree

Small firms are deeply interdependent, despite origin stories that center a single founder. Hoang and Antoncic’s (2003) review of network-based entrepreneurship research emphasizes how relationships affect access to information, resources, and opportunities. A dependable network can include employees, customers, other owners, lenders, professional advisers, family, community institutions, and suppliers. Its value depends on trust, diversity of knowledge, and the ability to exchange difficult information as well as the number of contacts.

Leaders frequently ask their network for encouragement when what they need is disconfirmation. A useful advisory circle contains people who understand the purpose of the enterprise and are willing to release current assumptions. Questions can be designed to invite evidence: “What would make this fail?” “Whose labor is missing from this calculation?” “How do customers’ actions compare with their statements?” “Which commitment would be hardest to reverse?” Clear requests reduce the social ambiguity of helping and make it easier for others to contribute specific expertise.

The same principle applies inside the firm. Psychological safety is a shared belief that interpersonal risk taking is safe; it supports candor while leaving adoption decisions open (Edmondson, 1999). In a five-person business, the cost of contradicting the owner can feel especially high because authority, identity, and relationships overlap. Leaders can lower that cost by naming uncertainty, asking for contrary data before stating a preference, separating error review from blame, and rewarding early signals. A cashier who reports that customers misunderstand a promotion or a technician who challenges a rushed schedule may be protecting both the firm and its purpose.

### Convert uncertainty into bounded learning

A small experiment is a distinct learning design: an agreement about what is being tested, what evidence would change a decision, how much loss is acceptable, and when the team will review the result. This discipline keeps action from becoming motion. It also makes communication reciprocal: customers know what is provisional, employees know what feedback matters, and partners know the limits of the commitment.

A short decision record can protect that learning from hindsight. Before acting, the team writes what it currently believes, which evidence is strongest, which uncertainty matters most, and what outcome would prompt continuation, revision, or stopping. At review, people compare the result with the prior reasoning and treat the outcome as one important input. This helps distinguish a sound choice followed by bad luck from a weak choice rescued by luck. It also gives quieter team members a stable point of entry: they can question an assumption on the page first, leaving the leader’s identity and memory outside the initial exchange. The record gains value when it names whose knowledge shaped the decision and whose exposure carries the downside, making participation and risk distribution visible. Over time, the record reveals recurring blind spots and capabilities that deserve further investment.

Weick’s (1984) account of small wins helps explain why bounded action can build momentum. A concrete, completed outcome—ten repeat orders at a sustainable margin, a new handoff that reduces rework, a supplier agreement that lowers volatility—can reduce abstraction and generate allies. The win should be treated as situated evidence. Its value is informational as well as motivational. It shows what worked under particular conditions and what the next experiment can examine.

Resourcefulness is disciplined adaptation under constraint: seeing more possible uses in what is available, drawing knowledge from relationships, protecting a tolerable downside, and learning in public with the people who share the consequences. Scarcity remains real. The leader’s achievement is to keep scarcity from narrowing attention so completely that support, alternatives, and humane limits disappear.
