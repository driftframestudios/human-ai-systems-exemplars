# The Provisional Self at Work

### Becoming can proceed by trial

A person entering a changed work life may wait to feel authentic before acting. Yet authenticity during transition is rarely a fully formed inner truth waiting to be expressed. It can be discovered through experiments. Ibarra’s (1999) study of professionals adapting to more senior roles describes provisional selves: people observe possible models, try elements of those identities, and evaluate the results against internal standards and external feedback. The process is disciplined experimentation that integrates observation and invention.

Provisionality can relieve an unnecessary burden. The new manager can defer a permanent choice of leadership style while learning through the first month. The returning caregiver can let the relationship between work and family emerge through the new rhythm. The relocated professional can treat discomfort as data while trying a different way of participating. “For now, I am testing…” makes change specific enough to examine and temporary enough to revise.

Experiments should draw adaptable elements from several models. A leader may borrow one colleague’s way of framing decisions, another’s calm in conflict, and a third’s practice of crediting others, then adapt these behaviors to their own values and social position. Feedback should come from people who can see both impact and context. Useful feedback asks both “Did I look convincing?” and “Did this behavior make the work clearer, the relationship more reciprocal, and participation more possible?”

### Rewrite continuity while honoring loss

Major role changes disturb the story a person tells about who they are. Ibarra and Barbulescu (2010) argue that narrative identity work is especially prevalent in macro work-role transitions. Stories help connect past, present, and anticipated future, but they can also trap someone in a demand for perfect continuity: “I was always ambitious, therefore any new limit means decline,” or “I left this field, therefore my earlier expertise has lost value.”

A more generous story prizes accuracy over manufactured redemption. It can say that a period of care developed knowledge and also cost opportunities; that relocation was chosen and involved grief; that promotion is welcome and relationships have become more complicated; that illness changed capacity while preserving contribution. Complexity makes the account more credible. It also creates several points of connection between prior strengths and present needs.

Self-compassion can support this honest appraisal. In a series of experiments, Breines and Chen (2012) found that self-compassion increased several forms of self-improvement motivation after weakness, transgression, or failure relative to comparison conditions. The studies offer bounded evidence from experimental settings: self-compassion can support growth. Broader workplace application awaits further study. A transitioner can recognize an awkward meeting, missed detail, or depleted week while keeping the appraisal specific.

Compassionate review has three movements: describe what happened accurately, locate the event in its real conditions, and identify one repair or experiment. It balances accountability with care. “I entered the conversation before learning the decision history; I will ask for the prior memo and confirm the purpose before the next meeting” is both kind and actionable because it treats readiness as developmental.

### Give intention a time and place

Identity experiments become developmental when they enter behavior. Implementation intentions link a cue with a response—an “if–then” plan—and a meta-analysis found that such plans can improve goal attainment across varied domains (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006). For a person in transition, the method can reduce cognitive load at recurring pressure points.

If a conversation turns to after-hours availability, then the returning employee will state the protected boundary and offer two daytime routes. If the new leader notices they are giving an answer before hearing the team, then they will ask one clarifying question and invite the closest-to-the-work person first. If the relocated worker leaves a meeting confused by a local reference, then they will ask their onboarding partner that day and turn uncertainty into a question. The plan is small enough to enact and meaningful enough to shift a pattern.

These plans need environmental support. Boundary statements require managers who honor them; requests for context build learning when colleagues respond constructively. Reliable allies can rehearse language, accompany a difficult conversation, confirm what was agreed, and intervene when power is uneven. Social support is most useful when its form matches the stressor (Cohen & Wills, 1985). Sometimes encouragement is right; sometimes the person needs information, coverage, advocacy, or a door opened by someone with more authority.

### Let progress accumulate on an open horizon

Transitions invite premature endings. The person is declared “fully back,” “settled,” or “ready” while the private work of adjustment continues. A developmental view allows competence to return unevenly. One routine stabilizes before another; one relationship becomes safe while another remains uncertain; confidence in a core task arrives before comfort with the culture.

Job crafting can provide modest room for agency as people alter task, relational, or cognitive boundaries (Wrzesniewski & Dutton, 2001). The new employee may cluster demanding work around periods of reliable energy, deepen a relationship with a complementary colleague, or reconnect an administrative task to the people it serves. Opportunity for such changes varies with job design, and managers retain responsibility for sound design. Where discretion exists, crafting can help a role become inhabitable and sustainable.

Career adaptability includes curiosity and confidence alongside concern and control (Savickas & Porfeli, 2012). Curiosity keeps several possible selves available; confidence grows from tested action; concern connects present experiments to a future; control locates the choices that genuinely belong to the person. Each can operate amid uncertainty. Their value is in supporting movement while the final shape remains open.

The provisional self is a developing self, learning in relationship with prior experience, new demands, trusted people, feedback, and material limits. Realistic hope comes from this plurality. There can be more than one honorable way to contribute, more than one arrangement of work and life, and more than one next step after a difficult week. A transition may culminate in a more spacious account of competence, belonging, and change.
