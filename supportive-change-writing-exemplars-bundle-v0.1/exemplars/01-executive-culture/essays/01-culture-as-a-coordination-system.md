# Culture as a Coordination System

### What culture coordinates

An executive team communicates through far more than speeches. It communicates through the issues that receive meeting time, the exceptions it tolerates, the people it promotes, and the losses it is willing to name. Employees read these choices as evidence about what the organization is actually trying to do. That reading matters acutely during transformation, as established routines lose explanatory power and information remains unevenly distributed. Culture then becomes a working answer to four questions: What is happening? Who belongs in interpreting it? What may we try? What will happen if an attempt reveals a problem?

This view changes the executive task. Culture develops socially as people compare messages with experience and make plausible accounts that support action (Weick, Sutcliffe, & Obstfeld, 2005). Gioia and Chittipeddi’s (1991) study of strategic change showed executive sensegiving and organizational sensemaking as reciprocal processes in place of a one-way cascade. Leaders offered an interpretation; members responded, revised, and sometimes redirected it. Strategic authority remains intact, while execution depends on whether people can place a choice within a coherent account and contribute knowledge beyond senior leaders’ field of view.

Transformation therefore starts with interpretive honesty. Executives can describe what is known, what remains uncertain, what constraints are real, and what evidence would change the present view. Such clarity leaves difficulty and loss visible while reducing the cognitive tax of deciphering euphemism. Research on change reactions suggests that people respond to a change’s content, process, anticipated benefit or harm, and the context that preceded it (Oreg, Vakola, & Armenakis, 2011). A credible account makes room for those appraisals. It treats concern as information about consequences, identity, or feasibility rather than as a character flaw.

### From announcement to reciprocal meaning

Readiness is sometimes mistaken for enthusiasm. A more useful conception includes beliefs about why change is needed, whether the proposed response is appropriate, whether people can contribute to it, and whether leaders will provide support (Armenakis, Harris, & Mossholder, 1993). This is why repetition alone rarely resolves skepticism. A message becomes actionable when employees can test it against local reality and receive a serious response. Communication is reciprocal when a question can alter an explanation, a warning can alter a sequence, and expertise can alter a decision.

Employee voice grows from evidence that an open door is usable. In a large field study, managerial openness was more consistently related to improvement-oriented voice than broad transformational behavior, partly through employees’ judgments about interpersonal risk (Detert & Burris, 2007). The result is intuitively demanding: an executive’s inspiring account may invite identification while still leaving dissent dangerous. Psychological safety is the shared sense that interpersonal risks such as admitting uncertainty, asking for help, or reporting error will receive respectful, accountable handling. It has been associated with learning behavior in teams, especially when contextual support and leader coaching are present (Edmondson, 1999). It combines interpersonal candor with clear accountability so inconvenient information can enter the work.

Status makes this condition uneven. Nembhard and Edmondson (2006) found that inclusive leader behavior helped health-care teams cross professional status boundaries and engage in improvement. At executive scale, the lesson is to examine who can use a channel at a reasonable personal cost. Contractors, employees in declining units, people carrying stigmatized identities, and middle managers caught between targets may interpret the same invitation differently. Practical inclusion begins with hearing and closes the loop by acknowledging what was understood, explaining what remains fixed and what remains open, and making visible how input affected action.

### Capability grows through supported action

People are more likely to invest effort when they believe their action can produce an effect. Bandura (2000) described collective efficacy as a shared belief in a group’s capacity to organize and execute action. Distinct from self-esteem and motivational decoration, it develops through experience, including mastery, credible social modeling, and feedback. During transformation, this suggests a progression from abstract ambition to bounded opportunities in which teams can use existing expertise, combine it with new knowledge, and observe a consequence.

Small progress matters because it supplies evidence. Yet its value is easily distorted. An under-resourced symbolic pilot, or a local success that leaders cite while preserving contradictory incentives, may weaken confidence. Progress becomes capability-building when the problem is consequential, the team has discretion proportionate to responsibility, and learning can travel. Positive psychological resources have been associated with more supportive attitudes and adaptive behavior during organizational change, while coherent systems remain indispensable to interpretation of that evidence (Avey, Wernsing, & Luthans, 2008). Confidence is most credible when people can name the strengths already available—craft knowledge, trusted relationships, reliable routines—and when the organization supplies complementary resources.

Feedback is central to this process. It should include operational measures alongside signals about workload, understanding, belonging, and unintended harm. Rafferty and Griffin (2006) found that how employees perceived change—its frequency, impact, planning, and uncertainty—related to stress and coping. A dashboard that records delivery while omitting human strain provides an incomplete picture of execution. Conversely, an unanswered pulse survey can teach employees that disclosure is ceremonial. A mature feedback system joins measurement with interpretation and response.

### Progress with moral memory

Transformation redistributes more than tasks. It can redistribute status, autonomy, security, and access to opportunity. An organization therefore needs moral memory: the capacity to remember who absorbed earlier costs, which promises were deferred, and where trust was spent. Moral memory supports deliberate revision of legacy arrangements by acknowledging that people interpret new requests through prior experience. Compassion can be coordinated through ordinary organizational structures when attention, trust, and resources are redirected toward suffering (Dutton, Worline, Frost, & Lilius, 2006). Care becomes organizationally meaningful when tone leads to changes in access, timing, workload, or support.

Leaders also shape whether progress is experienced as collective. Celebrating a milestone can strengthen attention and motivation, but only if recognition corresponds to the distributed labor that produced it. The people who prevented failure, translated across functions, cared for customers through disruption, or helped colleagues learn are part of the achievement. Naming such contributions reinforces interdependence and belonging. It also counters the tendency to narrate change as a sequence of executive decisions followed by employee adoption.

The strongest change practices are mutually reinforcing: a coherent account makes participation intelligible; reciprocal voice improves the account; supported action builds efficacy; feedback exposes constraints; and fair recognition preserves the relationships needed for the next step. Research syntheses accordingly portray successful change as a sequence of evidence-informed activities rather than a single charismatic intervention (Stouten, Rousseau, & De Cremer, 2018). Outcomes remain contingent on markets, technology, politics, and chance. Together, these practices create a culture capable of learning while retaining direction.

Realistic hope emerges here as an organizational judgment. It says that difficulty can be named alongside agency, plans can be revised with dignity, and incremental gains deserve attention as partial evidence. Executives earn that hope when their signals, resources, and responses allow people to see themselves as members of a supported collective able to make the next answer better.
