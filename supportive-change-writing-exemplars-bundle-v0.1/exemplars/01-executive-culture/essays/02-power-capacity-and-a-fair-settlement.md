# Power, Capacity, and a Fair Settlement

### Make power visible in culture work

Culture is attractive to executives because it seems to join meaning and performance. It can also become a polite substitute for examining power. A stalled transformation may be diagnosed as insufficient openness, weak resilience, or attachment to the past when the more immediate causes are incompatible targets, concentrated decision rights, or credible fears of loss. The language of shared values can make unequal exposure appear communal: everyone is said to be “on the journey,” even though some people choose the destination while others surrender roles, schedules, status, or security.

Research on change recipients cautions against reducing reactions to a stable disposition. People’s responses vary with process, context, anticipated consequences, and the behavior of change agents (Oreg, Vakola, & Armenakis, 2011). A person who contests a timetable may be protecting safety or service quality. A middle manager who appears hesitant may be reconciling incompatible mandates. A group described as nostalgic may possess institutional memory about why an earlier effort failed. Each interpretation deserves critical investigation before executives convert disagreement into a psychological deficiency.

Power enters sensemaking because interpretations travel with unequal force. Senior leaders can name an event, repeat that name across channels, and attach resources to it. Others may know more about local consequences yet possess a smaller platform. Sensemaking is social, action-oriented, and politically charged (Weick, Sutcliffe, & Obstfeld, 2005). An evidence-aware account therefore asks who can define the situation, whose evidence is treated as strategic, and whose experience is relegated to implementation detail.

### Consequential voice earns trust

Organizations often respond to this problem by inviting voice. Influence becomes credible when listening sessions, surveys, or councils lead to an accountable response. Employees weigh both interpersonal safety and expected efficacy: Will speaking receive protection, and is it likely to matter? Morrison’s (2014) review shows that voice and silence arise from multiple judgments about risk, obligation, and utility. Repeated disclosures that leave problems untouched can teach silence through futility rather than reveal a failure of courage.

Leader behavior affects those judgments. Detert and Burris (2007) found managerial openness associated with improvement-oriented voice, while Nembhard and Edmondson (2006) showed how inclusive leadership could mitigate status barriers in health-care teams. Dependence persists even when an invitation feels warm. A worker whose schedule, evaluation, immigration status, contract renewal, or promotion rests with the recipient of criticism faces a different decision from a senior professional with external options. Executives should treat aggregate participation data cautiously when they obscure these asymmetries. Silence remains especially ambiguous where the cost of complaint is high.

Practical inclusion begins after the disclosure. It asks whether decision criteria become clearer, whether resources move, whether a harmful assumption is revised, and whether the person who raised the issue remains in good standing. Requests may lead to different judgments; each deserves a reasoned response and a route for continued challenge. When leaders explain tradeoffs and identify what evidence shaped the decision, they help convert consultation into institutional learning. Story collection earns trust when it serves that learning.

### Capacity precedes commitment

Transformation demands cognitive, emotional, and temporal work. People must understand new goals, learn unfamiliar practices, relinquish routines, maintain current service, and often support colleagues through uncertainty. Those demands are routinely omitted from business cases. A large German survey study found organizational change associated with psychosocial risks and poorer mental-health indicators, while emphasizing the limits of causal inference from its repeated cross-sectional design (Backhaus et al., 2024). The appropriate lesson is to give the occupational-health consequences of change explicit design attention.

Executive narratives frequently praise adaptability while leaving capacity fixed. The resulting gap is then absorbed through overtime, workarounds, and informal care. Bandura’s (2000) collective efficacy requires shared capacity for action; privatized system strain produces a different condition. Shared confidence grows when people have usable resources and evidence of joint influence. It decays when success depends on concealing exhaustion. Positive psychological resources may help individuals navigate uncertainty, while staffing, authority, and coherent priorities remain essential complements (Avey, Wernsing, & Luthans, 2008).

Care must therefore change material conditions. Dutton and colleagues (2006) showed how organizational structures could be repurposed to notice suffering and coordinate an effective response. Compassion joins interpersonal concern with a distributive dimension: whose burden is reduced, whose time is protected, and whose needs are treated as legitimate. During restructuring, humane intent becomes humane effect through a feasible handover period.

### Transformation as a fair settlement

A critical view can produce a demanding and useful definition of transformation: a provisional settlement among purpose, power, capability, and consequence. “Provisional” matters because every design leaves some effects unforeseen. “Settlement” matters because people need enough clarity and fairness to coordinate, even while revision remains possible.

Such a settlement starts by distinguishing fixed constraints from choices still open to influence. It makes losses discussable and visible alongside aspirational language. It recognizes groups whose contributions are essential but whose bargaining power is weak. It measures progress through adoption rates alongside indicators of workload, error, access, and trust. And it remembers that fairness is judged through outcomes, voice, explanation, and consistency.

Readiness research has long emphasized credibility, social dynamics, and beliefs about appropriateness and support (Armenakis, Harris, & Mossholder, 1993). Stouten, Rousseau, and De Cremer’s (2018) synthesis likewise presents organizational change as disciplined work across diagnosis, participation, leadership, and institutionalization. These traditions become stronger when joined to a political question: who possesses the means to enact or resist the proposed future? That question helps make participation consequential and optimism freely held.

Realistic hope is possible when the organization supplies reasons to believe that candor, adjustment, and mutual support can change the path. It allows employees to hold a full range of responses while leaders create trustworthy evidence: a conflict resolved with protection for the participants, a workload reduced when data reveal strain, a frontline warning incorporated before harm, and a milestone recognized alongside the remaining distance. Each instance is small relative to enterprise transformation. Together they reveal whether culture is a shared capacity for adaptation or simply the vocabulary through which authority describes itself.
