# Systems That Make Collaboration Possible

### Align Local Incentives With Collective Outcomes

A familiar organizational drama begins with leaders asking functions to collaborate while measuring them in ways that reward separation. Procurement is praised for lowering unit cost, operations for stability, product teams for speed, and sales for booked revenue. Each group may behave rationally within its scorecard and still create a fragmented customer experience. When the resulting conflict is diagnosed as a “silo mentality,” an institutional contradiction is recast as an attitude problem.

Cross-functional tension often contains genuine differences in knowledge, but it also reflects incentives, scarce resources, and the distribution of consequences. Carlile (2002) showed that knowledge boundaries become especially difficult when interests are at stake. A request carries both information and a potential transfer of budget, reputational exposure, or late work. Cronin and Weingart’s (2007) account of representational gaps helps explain why well-intentioned specialists can pursue incompatible versions of success and invites explicit attention to who has authority to impose a version on others.

The first task of collaboration is therefore diagnostic. Which outcomes are jointly owned? Which costs remain invisible because they fall outside the sponsoring function’s measures? Which deadlines became promises ahead of participation by those expected to deliver them? A shared goal becomes credible when governance and rewards make attention to that goal possible. Calls for partnership then rest on a design that supports collective action.

### Voice Is Shaped by Status and Consequence

Psychological safety is frequently invoked as though leaders can create it by welcoming questions. Edmondson (1999) defined and studied it as a group-level belief related to interpersonal risk that extends beyond friendliness. The risk varies with status. A senior expert may experience disagreement as spirited debate; a contractor, newcomer, or employee from a marginalized group may reasonably calculate that the same act threatens access, evaluation, or continued employment.

Nembhard and Edmondson (2006) found that leader inclusiveness could support engagement in improvement work across professional-status differences. Yet an invitation to speak is weak if decision processes consistently disregard the invited knowledge. A credible voice system closes the loop: it states who will decide, records how input affected the choice, explains the final judgment, and protects those who surface unwelcome facts. It also recognizes that silence may reflect learned caution alongside a wealth of unspoken ideas.

Communication protocols can help, though their reach remains limited against retaliation, employment insecurity, or unequal access to influential networks. When leaders ask for candor, they incur obligations: to protect people from punishment, distinguish challenge from disloyalty, and share the reputational cost of raising a problem. Care becomes inclusion when participation has a route to consequence.

### The Unequal Burden of Bridging

Boundary spanning is often praised as a leadership capability. Marrone’s (2010) review describes activities such as obtaining resources, representing a team, and coordinating tasks across its edges. These activities can improve access and adaptation, but they also create role strain. Connectors translate vocabulary, anticipate objections, maintain relationships, and soothe conflict—labor that is cognitively and emotionally demanding despite its frequent absence from project plans.

Organizations often allocate this work informally to those perceived as agreeable, socially skilled, or broadly connected. The result can be a hidden tax on people already expected to perform belonging for others. The same person is asked to mentor newcomers, interpret between functions, remember stakeholder histories, and rescue broken handoffs, all while being evaluated on individual output. Collaboration built on voluntary repair by a few conscientious employees becomes fragile and unfair.

Fluid participation intensifies the problem. Mortensen and Haas (2018) argue that contemporary teams are often overlapping and loosely bounded. Under these conditions, calendars fill with coordination while the architecture of coordination often lacks clear ownership. The organization benefits from flexible access to expertise, while individuals carry switching costs and ambiguous obligations. Goodwill may delay failure, yet finite attention also requires capacity and prioritization.

### Decision Rights Are a Form of Communication

Many apparent communication failures are unresolved questions of authority. People exchange updates repeatedly because ownership remains unclear for trading schedule against scope, accepting risk, or sending a local choice to system-level review. Okhuysen and Bechky (2009) identify accountability, predictability, and common understanding as integrating conditions of coordination. Clear decision rights contribute to all three. They tell participants who acts, whose knowledge must enter before action, and where an appeal can go.

This clarity should preserve distributed authority. Expertise coordination works when relevant knowledge is recognized and mobilized (Faraj & Sproull, 2000). A sound arrangement places routine choices near the work while reserving genuinely cross-system tradeoffs for a forum with the authority and information to make them. It defines thresholds for escalation, time limits for consultation, and conditions that reopen a decision. In other words, governance itself becomes a feedback system.

Shared artifacts can expose whether governance is real. A decision record that names affected groups, consulted expertise, assumptions, and review dates makes omission visible. A dependency map can show when one function receives risk in excess of its resources. These tools gain value by creating institutional memory and reducing the ease with which power can rewrite how a commitment was made.

### Redesign Before You Rally

Evidence on teams supports investments in information sharing, shared cognition, and relational coordination (DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus, 2010; Gittell, 2002; Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009). These relational and cognitive resources become durable when capacity, incentives, and authority align with them. Effective change revises bonus systems that reward unilateral commitments, supplies enough staffing for response, and treats each group’s sacrifices and constraints as part of the shared purpose.

System repair begins by tracing recurrent friction to its production conditions. If every launch produces a late compliance crisis, move compliance knowledge and decision gates earlier. If operations repeatedly inherit unsupported features, give operational cost standing in portfolio choices. If a single liaison holds the partnership together, create role clarity, backup, and workload credit. If joint work succeeds only through exceptions, revise the rule.

The deepest form of collaboration is institutional honesty: acknowledging interdependence in the way work is funded, measured, scheduled, and governed. Every design meets unforeseen contingencies, so relationships remain essential. When the system carries its share of the coordinating burden, people can direct trust toward learning and invention as the organization resolves its contradictions.
