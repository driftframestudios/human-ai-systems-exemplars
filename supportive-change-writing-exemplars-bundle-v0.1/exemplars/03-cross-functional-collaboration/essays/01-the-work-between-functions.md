# The Work Between Functions

### Difference Is the Asset and the Difficulty

Organizations divide work into functions because specialization creates depth. Finance becomes fluent in exposure and return; operations in flow and reliability; sales in customers and commitments; technology in architectures and failure modes. The value of a cross-functional group lies in bringing these locally developed forms of knowledge to a problem that requires several functions. Yet the same division that creates expertise also produces partial views. A launch date may appear to marketing as a promise, to engineering as a probability distribution, to compliance as a threshold, and to service as a future queue. The disagreement may arise from a collision among legitimate representations of the work.

Cronin and Weingart (2007) call such differences “representational gaps”: people may believe they are debating solutions while actually working from different descriptions of the problem. In an ethnographic study of product development, Carlile (2002) similarly found that knowledge is localized, embedded in practice, and invested with consequences. Translation becomes harder when a proposed change redistributes effort or risk. These findings support treating alignment as an unfolding process. A team must first surface what each function sees, what it stands to gain or lose, and which dependency is being negotiated.

This approach preserves specialization. Coordinating expertise depends partly on knowing where knowledge resides and bringing it to bear at the right time (Faraj & Sproull, 2000). Transactive memory research describes a related capacity: effective groups develop credible knowledge of “who knows what,” allowing members to specialize while remaining connected (Lewis, 2003). The practical aim is sufficient mutual legibility. Each contributor should be able to explain both a recommendation and the constraint, uncertainty, or downstream consequence that makes it reasonable.

### Give Interdependence a Visible Form

Abstract appeals to “work as one team” rarely show people where their work touches. A shared artifact can. A service blueprint, dependency map, decision log, customer journey, prototype, risk register, or scenario is useful when it gives different specialists something they can inspect and change together. Carlile (2002) describes such boundary objects as means of representing, learning about, and transforming knowledge across domains. Their value lies in locating ambiguity in a form that people can question together.

A good boundary object preserves consequential difference. A project plan that displays only milestones may hide uncertainty; a dashboard that collapses all risk into a single color may imply consensus while views remain divided. By contrast, an artifact can show confidence ranges, ownership, assumptions, affected groups, and unresolved choices. It can make clear that a “small” feature request creates substantial testing work, or that a technically elegant solution places an unreasonable burden on frontline staff. The object becomes a place where care is operational: people can see who absorbs the cost of a decision and invite those people into the decision before commitment hardens.

Coordination research suggests that plans, routines, meetings, and schedules work by supporting accountability, predictability, and common understanding (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009). Those conditions are more informative than a preference for any particular tool. A daily meeting that leaves ownership diffuse is ceremony. A modest shared document that reveals a hidden dependency may be transformative. The question to ask of any coordinating mechanism is simple: what greater capacity to anticipate, understand, or answer for does it create?

### Build a Reciprocal Communication System

Information sharing is positively associated with team performance, but the evidence also suggests that sharing is most valuable when information is uniquely held and relevant to the task (Mesmer-Magnus & DeChurch, 2009). The better goal is communication that is timely, selective, and reciprocal. A sender should state what changed, why it matters, what remains uncertain, and what response is needed. A receiver should be able to paraphrase the consequence, challenge an assumption, and signal the conditions required for action.

Reciprocity also depends on interpersonal conditions. Edmondson’s (1999) research linked psychological safety—shared confidence that interpersonal risk is tolerable—with learning behavior in work teams. It combines meaningful standards with practical permission to ask a basic question, disclose an error, or oppose a premature consensus while preserving standing and dignity. Leader inclusiveness matters especially where professional status differs; in health-care teams, words and actions that invited participation helped reduce the silencing effects of status (Nembhard & Edmondson, 2006).

The design implication is to distribute voice intentionally. Ask the function that will inherit the risk to speak before the function with the greatest formal authority. Separate a round of problem description from solution advocacy. Invite written input before a meeting when rapid conversation favors fluent or senior participants. Record dissent and the conditions under which a decision should be revisited. These measures improve the conditions for candor while retaliation remains a plausible constraint, and they make reciprocal communication less dependent on individual courage.

### Learn Through Intermediate Commitments

Novel work rarely yields to complete analysis in advance. Majchrzak, More, and Faraj (2012) observed cross-functional teams that generated intermediate scaffolds—provisional ideas that participants repeatedly modified—while some differences in specialist knowledge remained unresolved. Their finding complicates the assumption that deep mutual translation must always precede action. Sometimes a small, revisable object lets people learn enough from one another to keep creating.

This is the proper role of incremental progress. A pilot, mock-up, limited release, or rehearsal functions as both a bounded deliverable and a question posed to reality. The answer should return quickly to the people whose work and judgment shaped the trial. Team-cognition research finds meaningful relationships between shared cognitive structures, team processes, and performance (DeChurch & Mesmer-Magnus, 2010), while shared understanding remains continuously updated through action. Each cycle can clarify the problem, revise the map of expertise, and strengthen collective confidence through evidence of what the group can actually accomplish.

The scale of the commitment matters. Excessive scale invites defense of sunk costs; trivial scale yields little learning. A useful intermediate commitment is bounded, observable, safe enough to revise, and close to the dependency under dispute. It carries a named hypothesis: if we change this handoff, what should improve, for whom, and what unintended burden should we watch?

### Sustain the Whole System

Cross-functional success can still be locally extractive. A project may meet its deadline because one coordinator absorbs after-hours translation, one operational group takes on invisible maintenance, or one junior member continually repairs relationships among senior colleagues. Boundary-spanning work brings information and resources across group lines, but it also consumes attention and can expose people to conflicting demands (Marrone, 2010). In fluid, overlapping teams, participation itself becomes dynamic and boundaries less clear (Mortensen & Haas, 2018). Explicit protection gives dependable connectors sustainable roles, capacity, and backup.

Sustainable collaboration therefore includes capacity accounting. The team should name translation, liaison, and repair as work; rotate it where feasible; build redundancy around key relationships; and examine whose core responsibilities are displaced by shared initiatives. Interteam coordination is associated with later project outcomes in longitudinal research, especially when established early (Hoegl et al., 2004), and its resource demands require time, authority, and recognition.

At its best, cross-functional work does more than combine inputs. It creates a dependable social and cognitive infrastructure: people know whom to approach, how to express consequential difference, what evidence will change a decision, and how to repair a miss. Gittell’s (2002) research on relational coordination emphasizes shared goals, shared knowledge, mutual respect, and timely problem-solving communication. Those qualities preserve specialist boundaries while making the power and effort involved in crossing them explicit.
