Background · systems close to consequence

Complicated technology becomes useful when people can act on it together.

Twenty years of shipping products into regulated, high-stakes, frequently changing environments has shaped a practical habit: find what is actually being measured, distinguish signal from noise, and repair the control loop. Biomedical engineering supplied the first vocabulary. Biopharma, medical devices, genomics, logistics, insurance, and education supplied the lived constraints.

Most of the work has involved translating among business units, scientists, designers, engineers, quality partners, and executives without flattening what any of them know. The people who understand both the biology and the computation can explain what a system is doing—not merely what it promises.

I work best when I believe in an organization’s customer. That fit becomes clearer early when the team can inspect its evidence, constraints, and decision rights together.

What I Actually Deliver · decision before decoration

I find the problem behind the problem.

A global review platform asked for a redesign. Server logs, exploratory forecasting, and targeted usability work exposed a different constraint: desktop navigation was arriving in a collapsed mobile menu. People clicked body content and empty space while looking for routes that existed but remained difficult to find.

The redesign therefore became a system change. Top-level navigation improved information scent; a carefully bounded NLP support route addressed recurring questions; survey free text revealed adverse-event language that the existing taxonomy could not capture. One evidence base informed a UX repair, a reporting mechanism, and compliance work.

AI and ML · signal, context, handoff

Modern methods become useful when they preserve the route back to judgment.

A streaming detector can locate an unusual clickstream combination. A component-level comparison can show that a frequently rendered route receives unexpectedly little interaction. An AI-legible viewlet can carry the source, affected population, boundary, and named receiver into the next decision. Each method changes work; none replaces the people who interpret consequences.

Placement · solve where the consequence lives

I find where solutions actually belong.

While exploring AI phone support for a logistics company, the team found a more immediate gap: inbound Facebook messages—the channel actual purchasers were using—were going unseen. A chatbot aimed at the phone channel would have automated the wrong touchpoint. The useful move was to repair channel coverage and ownership first.

I also design problems out of existence when the system permits it. A durable fix changes the state space so a class of error becomes harder to create, easier to observe, or safe to recover. The result is more than a patched symptom: the people operating the system inherit a clearer routine.

The Stakes · ordinary moments can be consequential

Design connects people and technology at the moment they need each other.

A boarding pass that will not load at the gate and a bill-payment path buried in ambiguous links are familiar examples. In clinical, scientific, and operational systems, the same friction can separate a person from care, an analyst from the right evidence, or a team from a recoverable decision.

Design is important because access, interpretation, and recovery are material. I fit technology to the context of people and their work. The multidisciplinary range—from product research through full-stack engineering and quality collaboration—helps the team see where an interface ends and an operating system begins.

I am still learning new things every day. That is not a weakness in the posture; it is how a consequential system stays in contact with reality.
