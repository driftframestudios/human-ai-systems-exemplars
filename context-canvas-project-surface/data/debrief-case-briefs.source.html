Rebuilding the Genomic Viewer While the Work Continued

At-a-Glance

I was asked to redesign the experience and rearchitect the front end of Foundation Medicine’s FDA-regulated clinical-genomics viewer. My work spanned UX research, UX/UI design, React with TypeScript, and Java services on Spring—connecting clinical workflows with the systems that supported them.

Situation and stakes
A researcher-built GWT application from around 2010 had grown into a core clinical-genomics platform used across the company. By 2019, it needed an overhaul. Interruption delayed time-sensitive treatment insights for physicians and the cancer patients they served.
Scope and role · Q1 2019 · Q3 2022–Q1 2025
I owned the UX/UI as Principal Product Designer & Software Engineer III on a core seven-person delivery group within a 50-plus-engineer Computational Biology organization. I led requirements gathering with cross-functional stakeholder groups ranging from scientists to executives. I mentored on UX/UI by delegating specific sessions to other engineers.
Challenges
Technical debt accumulated in tacit workarounds through Excel and internal tools. Key interactions went unmeasured by existing system instrumentation, and the primary user group was running interference outside the engineering team's visibility.
Key decision
I centered participatory design sessions around functional prototypes. I modeled profiles, permissions, and status first and iterated live during sessions. Design concepts were then translated into bounded typed components for continual refinement.
Results
The team delivered more than a redesign. New dashboard views brought operational analytics together, saving over 20 minutes per person previously spent on daily SQL queries and emails. New React.js front-end, custom MUI components, and bespoke Node.js tooling ran parallel as iterations continued.
What this enables
My profile and permission work gave qualified people the right cases, reduced errors, and supported later automatic assignment. My schema-based structured-note work also made human interventions countable across candidates for tiered automation.

Basis and limits: I designed and implemented the delivered interfaces and services within a purposeful partial migration; complex Spring transformations, DTOs, database mediation, and portions of the underlying stack remained legacy-owned.

Solution and implementation quick scan

Solution Track

  1. Debrief. At FMI, rows in the database represent people with cancer. I was responsible for redesigning the UX/UI of a genomic viewer while new company agreements and advances in oncology continued to expand the existing application. I ran participatory design sessions with stakeholders to make genomics, clinical, and engineering decisions visible in one frictionless application experience.
  2. Bottleneck.
    • The legacy MVP tech stack tightly coupled a user's available tools with specific screens. Users were constantly context switching when assessing quality control metrics and underlying cancer biology.
    • Nuanced failure states, manual SQL queries, Slack, email, and macro-based Excel trackers distributed work state across people and tools.
    • Broad authentication lacked assay-, jurisdiction-, profile-, and action-level rules. My initial prototypes were too high-fidelity and made participants feel like the train had left without them onboard.
  3. Intervention.
    • I made profile, permission, assignment, case state, and recovery visible in every view in the interface.
    • I worked on the operational layers users voted most important through a gradual migration, then translated participant language into testable scenarios through Specification by Example. Every design library specifications was bundled with real application state.
  4. Outcome.
    • All recurring assignment errors were eliminated through a templated, transparent process for automatically assigning cases to users.
    • Custom profile and permission assignment rules, combined programmatic case notes, enabled managers to granularly override automatic assignment to respond to short-term changes in workflows or sudden priorities directly in the UI.
    • I structured notes from upstream teams separately from those destined for downstream teams, which enabled new kinds of telemetry.
    • 70–100 clinical users saved 20–30 minutes per person daily. 900+ non-clinical users now have new ways to see operational data, which meant they no longer needed to email the clinical users with questions.
  5. Repeatability.
    • Profile-first decomposition of workflows continued to guide the work as screens, services, and organizational conditions changed – even if the company reorgs, some logic has to gate access to cases based on jurisdiction.
    • Specification by Example's GIVEN-WHEN-THEN format enabled me to sit with users and draft clear, precise logic in isolation. I could then bundle the results into a single, reusable library of test scenarios.

Implementation Track

  1. Debrief. I used React, TypeScript, MUI, and Storybook to open a familiar contribution path into a GWT and Java estate, building the visual system, core tables, forms, operating modes, and shared component library.
  2. Bottleneck. GWT-to-React interop concentrated lifecycle, validation, DTO, payload, and rendering risk; I retired a widely liked global Masonry treatment when its coupling obstructed a stable capability transfer.
  3. Intervention. The exploding table, permission ledger, floating drawer, record-local collaboration, Case view, and Automated Advice put evidence, action, authorization, and state at the point of judgment while server checks retained access authority.
  4. Outcome. A shared dashboard replaced a daily SQL-and-email reconstruction that exceeded thirty minutes, service reads and structured transmission fields replaced tracker jobs, and intervention counts exposed candidates for tiered automation.
  5. Repeatability. Unit-Test-First, the Upstream Revision Loop, Storybook, and Testing Library connected scenarios, tests, components, and revisions so operating knowledge could survive team and platform change.

Bringing Clinical Trial Rules Into View

At-a-Glance

I worked with directors, Data Managers, biomarker specialists, and engineers to redesign how teams coordinated clinical trials at Foundation Medicine. I brought six working groups together to turn their knowledge into a new, intuitive interface.

Situation and stakes
A clunky UI slowed partner-trial work when connecting genomic profiling, study requirements, and biopharma data. Data Managers manually reconciled partner-specific formats and rules that governed clinical-trial decisions.
Scope and role · Q4 2022 – Q2 2023
As Senior Manager & Principal Designer of Experience Design, I owned the engagement end-to-end, designed the interface, and mentored mid-senior designers on engineering hand-off tactics.
Challenges
The organization was changing, intended users of the new system were still undecieded, and each group approached the work differently. The design had to make those differences workable before the future structure was known.
Key decision
Rather than wait for the reorganization to settle, I designed a visual rule builder flexible enough to support both partner-study setup and biomarker investigation.
Results
Scientists gained reusable study templates anda visual interface for biomarker investigations. Engineering shipped the application in one quarter. Data Managers could set up three to five more cases per week, and measurable setup errors fell 50%.
What this enables
Reliable automation and AI depend on structured data, explicit rules, and clear boundaries. This work established a reusable rule model for clinical-trial coordination and the basis of prepopulated partner configurations and measurable error corrections two years later.

Basis and limits: In this case, making a clickable prototype for every rule permutation was prohibitively time-consuming. I made engineering-facing clickable prototypes, so the teams responsible for production implementation had clear depictions of every component state.

Solution and implementation quick scan

Solution Track

  1. Debrief. Foundation Medicine works with biopharma partners to develop targeted therapies and help people with cancer find relevant clinical trials based on their tumor’s genomic profile. I was asked to reimagine the internal software that enabled this work.
  2. Bottleneck.
    • So many teams were involved, the only concrete resolution to meetings was often to schedule other meetings.
    • Three competing futures—a standalone product, a wider internal suite, and a code-first workflow—carried different ownership and handoff models.
    • Partner mappings and study rules were distributed across SharePoint, meetings, and careful manual work.
  3. Intervention.
    • I made clickable prototypes to focus meetings on disagreements and reducing the cost of friction.
    • From all the ways my prototypes were wrong, I designed an interface that gave leaders a concrete view of how the teams’ future work could connect.
    • I synthesized feedback into interfaces for teams to build and reuse study rules so biomarker investigation and partner-trial teams could check, correct, template, and extend the same workflows.
  4. Outcome.
    • Engineering built the design in one quarter.
    • Unexpected bonus: Account Managers gained the ability to define trial standards directly with biopharma partners.
    • Data Managers set up three to five more cases each week with a measured 50% error reduction and about ten minutes saved per setup.
  5. Repeatability. Make disagreements visible early, then give people a realistic prototype they can correct. Design the rules and relationships to support today’s work and the next set of questions.

Implementation Track

  1. Debrief. An aging Angular 2 application was moving toward an unsettled React implementation. Stale state could associate a population with the wrong partner, cohort, or study, so state transitions carried data integrity and governance ramifications.
  2. Bottleneck. Policy fit, broad access, structured response capture, machine-readable aggregation, and shared manipulation lived across separate tools, creating a precise need for a minimal connective foundation.
  3. Intervention. I built an SVG and XHTML worksheet, an Adobe UXP, and modern JavaScript components. I made LucidChart custom shapes, a DynamoDB-backed submission endpoint, and the rule builder component grammar so stakeholders could interact with something real as I designed.
  4. Outcome. The engineering team built the design in a single quarter with minimal revisions from me as a designer. I only updated the packet once to support a new assay product's unique workflow.
  5. Repeatability. Importing fully interactive SVG shapes into Figma speeded up both design and development. Stakeholders could see the interactions in familiar tools like LucidChart, other teams reused the shapes, and engineering inherited the states, dispatches, and error paths to guide component build-out.

At-a-Glance

Situation and stakes

Challenges
Vendor-feature comparison fragmented workflow states, instruments, and approval pathways. Design sessions needed mediums familiar enough for lab teams to revise together.

Reduce the full-suite ambition to a pilot. Use throwaway prototypes to describe vendor features against their API documentation.
Results
All candidate vendors were rated with the System Usability Scale and a weighted evaluation matrix. A vendor was selected, and the new LIMS/ELN shipped to production a couple of business quarters later.
What this enables
Teams can reuse participatory specifications across vendor demonstrations, cost comparison, automation, acceptance tests, and reversible migration.

Basis and limits: My prototypes made the scale of a bespoke build legible to executives and engineers at the beginning of the decision. Engineering groups retained responsibility for production implementation.

Solution and implementation quick scan

Solution Track

  1. Debrief. Foundation Medicine provides genomic profiling and related decision-support services used by oncology teams, researchers, and biopharma partners. Hundreds of lab personnel needed specimen identity, custody, state, review, and scientific meaning to remain coherent across sites and shifts.
  2. Bottleneck. Six-plus groups and eight-plus teams had forked their own models of samples, plates, reagents, runs, and sign-offs. Email, Slack, tickets, and separate systems carried the handoffs and failure recovery.
  3. Intervention. I made working prototypes to make disagreement inexpensive and turn participant corrections into vendor demonstrations, acceptance criteria, and automation tests. I protected my team's time by rebuffing requests for full specification suites.
  4. Outcome. Every candidate vendor received a SUS rating and weighted evaluation, one vendor was selected, and the new LIMS/ELN shipped to production a couple of business quarters later. My working prototypes based on actual open-source LIMS/ELN projects revealed the scope of a bespoke build before commitment.
  5. Repeatability. Teams can name shared objects and failures first, design in the open, adapt the carrier to the audience, translate scenarios into tests, and cost migration, validation, training, recovery, and operating change as part of the platform decision.

Implementation Track

  2. Bottleneck. Private data models split sample findability, sign-off history, movement, skips, recovery latency, verification, and deletion across systems. Slow loading and timeouts connected interface performance directly to costly rescanning, wasted reagents, and non-conformance reports.
  3. Intervention. I made working prototypes that mapped out shared objects, modeled lifecycle state, and made custody changes transactional. I prioritized vendors that captured instrument data at the source and had strong API implementations. Executable scenarios I drafted bridged from vendor analysis to engineering handoff.
  4. Outcome. Requirements and specification gathering unearthed critical bugs. Vendor demo goals slipped by one business quarter as the team converged on fixing problems that would still be present in the final product. The new LIMS/ELN shipped to production a couple of business quarters later and accounted for previously undocumented validation workflows.
  5. Repeatability. Executable scenarios and up-front migration costs travel between teams. Standing up a simple version based on open-source options is a quick way to make the true cost of building a bespoke version concrete.

Partial teaching result: the proposed design makes discrete findings and point-of-care context possible while the certified connection remains on a separate calendar.

How to Keep Patient Records Across Three EHRs

At-a-Glance

Situation and stakes

Challenges
Every EHR has its own local distribution, identity, tenant, workflow, and marketplace boundaries. Organization identity alone was too broad for safe multi-tenant activation.
Key decision
Keep one canonical order-and-result contract and choose each delivery path by distribution needs. Bind organization and tenant identity, then release through per-organization gates.
Results
Successful result: the result reached three EHR environments. A tenant-scoping caching misconfiguration was caught just before release.
What this enables
A shared clinical contract can scale through local conformance and activation rather than forcing several institutions into one operating contract. Remember that FHIR IDs are only unique within a single EHR system.

Basis and limits: This Debrief details publicly available standards and vendor behavior so that it is IP-free.

Solution track
